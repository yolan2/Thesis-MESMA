# Cosine similarity — concise thesis exposition

Cosine similarity quantifies the angular agreement between two real-valued vectors and is widely used when relative shape is the primary object of inference. Let \(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^n\); the cosine similarity is

$$
\mathrm{cos\_sim}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|_2\,\|\mathbf{b}\|_2\,}.
$$

This scalar measures the cosine of the angle between vectors: values close to one indicate near‑collinearity (very similar shape), values near zero indicate orthogonality (no shared structure), and negative values indicate opposing structure. Because the ratio normalizes by Euclidean norm, the measure is invariant to positive scalar changes in magnitude and therefore isolates pattern (shape) from brightness. This property makes cosine similarity especially appropriate for comparing temporal or spectral signatures when illumination or sensor gain vary across samples.

In the context of unmixing and endmember library construction, cosine similarity is used as a conservative diagnostic and pruning criterion: spectral‑temporal variants whose shape closely matches non‑vegetated references are considered spectrally ambiguous and, depending on operational thresholds, may be removed from the candidate library to reduce commission errors in fraction estimation. The decision rule is simple and parameterised by a single threshold on similarity; the threshold expresses an explicit trade‑off between retaining legitimate but edge‑case vegetation variants and removing spectra that are likely to be misattributed to vegetation in mixed pixels. A threshold near 0.70 is a practical default for multispectral, pentad‑aggregated time‑series because it removes clearly barren‑like shapes while preserving the majority of distinct vegetation phenologies; more conservative mapping objectives may justify thresholds in the 0.85–0.95 range.

For thesis methods and interpretation it is important to emphasise three points. First, the metric operates on shape rather than absolute reflectance, so any preprocessing that alters relative index amplitudes will change cosine values; state explicitly whether and how vectors were normalized in your pipeline. Second, the metric is insensitive to temporal warping and non‑linear misalignment — if phenological timing varies substantially between samples, consider complementary measures that permit temporal flexibility. Third, cosine similarity is not a probabilistic test: threshold choice should be defended empirically (sensitivity sweep against an independent loss such as out‑of‑bag unmixing error, and by inspection of representative spectra) and reported alongside uncertainty estimates for the mapping product.

Suggested figures to include in a thesis are (described here as prose rather than as implementation instructions): a labeled heatmap showing pairwise cosine similarity among representative library variants with vegetation classes annotated; a histogram of per‑variant maximum similarity to barren references with vertical lines marking the operational threshold(s); and a threshold‑sensitivity plot that relates the chosen cut to both the number of variants removed and an out‑of‑sample error metric. Captions should state the normalization strategy, the precise threshold used, and the quantitative impact on unmixing error.

A compact Methods paragraph suitable for inclusion in the main text reads well directly from this exposition: "Cosine similarity was used to assess shape‑level agreement between candidate endmember variants and non‑vegetated references. Spectral‑temporal vectors were L2‑normalized and compared by cosine; variants with maximum similarity to any barren reference above the operational threshold (default 0.70) were excluded from the library. Threshold choice was validated by a sensitivity analysis against out‑of‑sample unmixing error and by visual inspection of pairwise similarity structure."
